Before you lift

Every session, spend 5 minutes warming up before touching a weight. This wakes up the muscles and joints you're about to use and lowers injury risk.

  • 1–2 minutes of light movement — marching in place, jogging, or jumping jacks
  • 10 bodyweight squats
  • 10 arm circles each direction
  • 5 hip circles each direction
  • A few slow bodyweight reps of whatever the first exercise is that day, before adding weight

How to pick a starting weight

Since you're brand new to this, start lighter than you think you need to. The goal for week 1 is to learn the movement, not to struggle.

  • Pick a weight where the last 2–3 reps of a set feel challenging but you could still do 1–2 more with good form
  • If you can't finish the low end of the rep range (e.g. 10 reps), the weight is too heavy — drop it next set
  • If the high end of the rep range (e.g. 12 reps) feels easy the whole set, add weight next session
  • It's normal for different exercises to use very different weights — a shoulder press will always use less than a squat

How to pick a starting weight

Machines use a weight pin in a stack rather than picking up a dumbbell, but the same idea applies: start lighter than you think you need. The goal for week 1 is learning each machine's setup, not struggling against it.

  • Before adjusting the weight, always adjust the seat and pads to fit her body first — a machine sized wrong will feel harder or easier than it should regardless of the weight
  • Pick a pin position where the last 2–3 reps of a set feel challenging but she could still do 1–2 more with good form
  • If she can't finish the low end of the rep range (e.g. 10 reps), move the pin down one setting next set
  • If the high end of the rep range (e.g. 12 reps) feels easy the whole set, move the pin up one setting next session
  • It's normal for different machines to use very different weight numbers — a shoulder press machine will always show less than a leg press

Free weight setup basics

A little bit of gym-floor know-how makes the first few sessions much less intimidating.

  • Dumbbells are usually racked in ascending order by weight, printed on the end of each one
  • Grab both dumbbells of a pair from the same spot so you know they match — mismatched pairs happen more than you'd think
  • Always re-rack weights when you're done, even light ones — it's gym etiquette and keeps the area safe
  • If a movement calls for a bench, adjust it to flat (not incline) unless the exercise specifically says otherwise

Machine setup basics

Every machine at the gym has small adjustments — seat height, backrest angle, pad position — and getting these right matters as much as the weight itself.

  • Adjust the seat or backrest before touching the weight pin — most machines have a lever or knob near the seat
  • Look for a small sticker or diagram on the machine showing correct joint alignment (e.g., knee lined up with the pivot point) — most commercial machines have one
  • If a machine feels off — a joint gets pinched, or the resistance feels uneven — that's usually a setup issue, not a form issue. Don't push through it; ask a staff member to help adjust it
  • Move the weight pin fully in before pulling it out and repositioning — this avoids it snagging halfway

Go slow, on purpose

It's tempting to go all-in on day one, but strength training rewards patience in a way other things don't — pushing too hard too soon is the #1 reason beginners get hurt or burn out in week two.

  • Week 1 isn't about how much weight you lift — it's about learning what correct form feels like
  • Never add weight and reps in the same session; progress one at a time
  • A little muscle fatigue by the last rep is the goal, not exhaustion by rep 3
  • Resting a day between sessions isn't losing progress — it's part of how the muscle actually builds

Progress takes longer than it feels like it should

Visible changes are usually the last thing to show up, not the first. This is completely normal — it doesn't mean it isn't working.

  • Weeks 1–2: mostly nervous-system learning — the movements start feeling smoother and less awkward
  • Weeks 3–4: real strength gains — the same weight starts to feel lighter, or she can do a rep or two more
  • Around week 6 and beyond: this is usually when physical changes become noticeable
  • A session that felt "easy," a plateau week, or not seeing the scale move yet are not signs to quit — they're normal parts of the process

Stair Master (cardio)

This is optional, extra calorie burn on top of the lifting — done on the days you're not strength training, not stacked onto a lift day. Same rule as the lifting: start light and build up.

  • Aim for 2 sessions a week — 8–10 minutes in week 1, building to 15–20 minutes by week 4 (the tracker shows the target for whichever week you're on)
  • Keep it at a pace where you could hold a conversation but not sing — that's the effort level that's sustainable and actually gets used regularly
  • Stand tall and let your legs do the work — avoid leaning on the handrails, which quietly does the work for you
  • If a strength session already left your legs very fatigued, it's fine to skip cardio that day or swap in an easy walk instead

Form cues, exercise by exercise

Rest and breathing

  • Rest 60–90 seconds between sets — enough to recover, not so much that you cool down
  • Exhale on the hard part of the lift (standing up from a squat, pressing up), inhale on the easy part (lowering down)
  • Never hold your breath through a whole rep

When to stop or scale back

Muscle fatigue and mild soreness the next day are normal. Sharp pain, joint pain, or pain during a movement is not — stop that exercise for the day if it happens. If soreness from a session lasts more than 3–4 days, or something hurts even at rest, ease off and check in with a doctor or physical therapist before continuing that movement.
